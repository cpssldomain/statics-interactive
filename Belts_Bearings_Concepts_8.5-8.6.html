Belt and Bearing Friction — Concepts

A belt or rope wrapped on a fixed drum: the tension grows exponentially with the contact angle, T2 = T1eµβ. A shaft pressing on a collar or pivot: friction over the contact area resists rotation, M = (2/3)µP(R2³ − R1³)/(R2² − R1²). Move the sliders and watch where the numbers come from.

A flat belt on a fixed drum: T2 = T1eµβ

The belt is about to slip in the direction of T2 relative to the drum. Friction along the contact adds tension bit by bit, so the tension grows exponentially from T1 to T2. β is the contact angle in radians; the radius of the drum does not appear.

The belt is coloured by tension (light = T1, dark = T2). Arrow lengths are proportional to the tensions.
Tension along the contact: T(θ) = T1eµθ, θ from 0 to β.
2 · Section 8.5 · Fig. 8–18c

Where eµβ comes from: one belt element

An element subtending dθ carries T on one side and T + dT on the other, a normal force dN and a friction force dF = µ dN.

Belt element of length ds = r dθ. Friction opposes the slip of the belt relative to the drum, so the tension rises by dT.
ΣFx: T cos(dθ/2) + µ dN − (T + dT) cos(dθ/2) = 0
ΣFy: dN − (T + dT) sin(dθ/2) − T sin(dθ/2) = 0

With sin(dθ/2) ≈ dθ/2, cos(dθ/2) ≈ 1 and dT·dθ neglected:

µ dN = dT and dN = T dθ ⇒ dT/T = µ dθ
∫T1T2 dT/T = µ∫0β dθ ⇒ ln(T2/T1) = µβ ⇒ T2 = T1eµβ
The normal force per unit angle is T itself (dN = T dθ): a tighter belt presses harder, so it gets more friction — that is why the growth is exponential.
3 · Section 8.5 · Problems 8–86, 8–103

Hold or lift? The same rope works both ways

A load W hangs from a rope wrapped n turns on a fixed peg. To hold it, the rope tends to slip toward the load, so W = T2 and you pull only W e−µβ. To lift it, the rope tends to slip toward you, so you pull W e+µβ.

Bars on a logarithmic scale: pull to hold, the load, pull to lift.
4 · Section 8.6 · Figs. 8–20, 8–21 · Eqs. 8–7, 8–8

Collar and pivot bearings: M = µP reff

An axial force P presses the collar on a ring of radii R1 to R2. With uniform pressure p = P/π(R2² − R1²), each element gives dF = µp dA at arm r, so M = ∫r dF. The result is µP times an effective radius between R1 and R2; for a pivot (R1 = 0) it is 2R/3.

Contact ring (tan) seen at an angle, with an element dA and its friction dF (green). Dashed purple circle: reff.
5 · Section 8.6 · Example 8.9

The pressure distribution matters

Eqs. 8–7 and 8–8 hold only for uniform pressure. For the same axial force, pressure concentrated near the centre gives a smaller moment, pressure near the rim a larger one. For a pivot of radius R: M = k µPR.

Pressure p(r) across the pivot (scaled so every case carries the same P). The moment weights each ring by r².
Example 8.9 is the 1-D version: a bar of length 2a with pressure falling linearly from the centre gives M = µWa/3; with uniform pressure it would be µWa/2.

Procedure

  1. Direction of slip. Decide which way the belt or rope tends to slip relative to the fixed surface. The tension on that side is T2 (larger).
  2. Contact angle β in radians; add 2π for every full turn. Use µs for impending slip, µk for slipping.
  3. Free pulleys keep the tension the same on both sides; apply T2 = T1eµβ only at fixed drums, pegs and bands.
  4. Bearings: find P from equilibrium, then M from Eq. 8–7 (collar) or 8–8 (pivot) for uniform pressure. Check radii versus diameters.
  5. Non-uniform pressure: find the peak pressure from ΣF = 0 first, then integrate dM = r µ p dA.
